\section{Setting and threat model}\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Swarm.} $N$ agents answer a stream of questions $t=1,2,\dots$. On each question every agent broadcasts one answer. Receiver $i$ hears its own answer and a subset of its peers' answers (visibility $p_{\text{obs}}$), and must output a final answer using only what it has heard on this and earlier questions. No labels are ever revealed. A fraction $f$ of the agents is Byzantine; the rest are honest. Every honest agent is a receiver, and we score the mean accuracy over honest receivers. Byzantine receivers are never scored.

\paragraph{What the receiver knows.} One thing: it is itself honest, i.e.\ it reports its own best answer. It does not know $f$, who the attackers are, which model produced a peer's answer, or any label. Self-reported confidence is ignored throughout. It can be forged, and the original study showed a falsified self-report falls below chance~\citep{das2026aip}.

\paragraph{Adversary.} Byzantine agents may (i) know the gold answer, (ii) be \emph{rushing}, seeing the honest broadcasts on the current question before speaking, (iii) coordinate arbitrarily with each other, (iv) know the defence and its published constants, and (v) change behaviour over time. \Cref{tab:attacks} lists the strategies we evaluate. They are grouped by the assumption of our method each one attacks. Nine are symbolic. Four replay answers that real LLMs wrote when prompted to deceive~\citep{das2026aip}. Three more (\cref{sec:live}) come from live inference in this work.
