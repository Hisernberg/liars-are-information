\section{Where RACE fails, and other limitations}\label{sec:limits}

Theorem~\ref{thm:silence} leaves the adversary three levers. We found each one working against \race.

\paragraph{Violating conditional independence: camouflage.} The camouflage attacker tells the truth whenever the honest vote is clear, earning a high estimated accuracy. When the honest vote is contested, it coherently backs the strongest wrong honest answer. Its reports depend on the honest reports, not only on the truth, so the one-coin model misreads it as an expert. At $f=0.7$ it pushes \race\ \zooCamoRaceGap\ points below the receiver alone. Capping every peer's accuracy at the receiver's own (\race+self-cap) recovers part of the loss. No deployable method we tested beats the receiver alone under this attack at $f=0.7$. The obvious repair, fitting channels only on questions with disagreement (\raceD, \cref{sec:budget}), helps at $f=0.7$ but hurts at lower $f$ and against echo attackers. A channel model conditioned on the honest vote pattern, rather than a filter on questions, remains open.

\paragraph{Violating stationarity: sleepers.} A sleeper that behaves honestly on history and lies afterwards defeats every static fit, including the known-channel oracle, which is fitted on the same history (\cref{fig:zoo}). The causal online study (\cref{sec:online}) shows that refitting with forgetting recovers, with a lag set by the forgetting rate.

\paragraph{Weak anchors.} Identification requires the receiver to be better than chance on the \emph{effective} answer space (\cref{prop:switching}). On open-answer questions the candidate set is often just \{consensus, one alternative\}. There a receiver near 50\% is effectively at chance. A rushing attacker that lies with that receiver's own wrong answers then produces a labelling that is observationally equivalent to the truth from that receiver's viewpoint. In our MATH-500 runs this affects the weakest roster member (Llama-3.2-3B, 44.5\% on MATH-500). Stronger receivers in the same swarm are unaffected. \Cref{fig:competence} quantifies the gain as a function of receiver competence. It is also why \race\ trails \aip\ and majority on MATH-500 at small $f$.

\paragraph{Option-biased channels, and a post-hoc amendment.} The one-coin model gives each channel one accuracy and spreads its errors evenly over the alternatives. The live swarm (\cref{sec:live}) showed that small models violate this on binary questions: some answer ``yes'' or ``no'' largely regardless of the truth. There the frozen v3.0 fell below the receiver alone (\liveBoolqLlmVthreeFive\% vs.\ \liveBoolqLlmSelfFive\% at $f=0.5$). We amended \race\ to use class-conditional channels on binary questions (v3.1, \cref{sec:binary}), which scores \liveBoolqLlmRaceFive\% there. On the \binaryReplayCells\ replayed BoolQ cells it is never significantly worse than v3.0 (\binaryReplayWins\ significant gains, \binaryReplayLosses\ losses). The amendment was chosen after seeing the live data, so the E8 BoolQ result is not a confirmation of v3.1. The pre-registered fresh run E10 is: on BoolQ items the live models had never seen, v3.1 scores \hypHOneATarget\% against \hypHOneABase\% for v3.0 and \hypHOneBBase\% for the receiver alone (\cref{sec:live2}). Even with v3.1, the strongest live BoolQ receiver loses by pooling with a near-chance crowd (\liveGraniteSelf\% alone, \liveGraniteRace\% with \race).

\paragraph{Scope.} All replay studies use cached single-turn answers. Receivers aggregate final answers, not reasoning, and the symbolic attackers are gold-aware by design. The live study (\cref{sec:live}) uses small 1--2B models on a CPU and 120 questions per benchmark, so it is a confirmation, not a benchmark. Open-answer scoring relies on an approximate symbolic equivalence checker. Correlated honest errors (shared attractors) remain partly unmodelled: clone tempering only removes near-duplicates. The known-channel oracle shows the remaining headroom.
